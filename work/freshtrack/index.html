The brief

My MSc dissertation asked for a complete new business plan: a real opportunity, evidenced by external analysis and primary research, with a defined customer, a value proposition and a viable business model. I chose a problem I was living with. Students throw away food they paid for, then run short of money before the end of the month.

I treated it as a product problem as much as a business one. Every feature in the concept had to trace back to something the research showed, and the plan was awarded a First.

The problem

UK households waste £14 billion of food a year, and students, the group with the least financial resilience, feel it most. 96% are making cutbacks to cope with the cost of living, and student food bank use has doubled since 2021.

90% of people know food waste is a problem. Only 67% do anything about it.

That awareness-action gap became the core of the brief. Students don't need persuading that waste is bad. They need something that makes the right thing the easy thing.

The market context

Before designing anything I ran a full PESTLE analysis to test whether the timing was right. It was: policy, economics and social trends all pointed the same way.

  • P Political Highly favourable

    A government target to halve food waste by 2030, backed by £31m of innovation funding.

  • E Economic Strong driver

    96% of students making cutbacks. 74% of consumers interested in money-saving apps.

  • S Social Highly favourable

    2.86m UK students. Gen Z leads sustainable buying: 55% vs 13% of Baby Boomers.

  • T Technological Mixed

    98% of students own a smartphone, but apps lose 77% of users within three days.

  • L Legal Compliance cost

    GDPR for financial data (an estimated £340k), plus allergen rules for recipe suggestions.

  • E Environmental Major driver

    £14bn of household food waste a year, and 42% of Gen Z already track their carbon footprint.

Two findings became design constraints rather than background. Apps lose most users within three days, and students prefer simple apps over feature-rich ones. Whatever I designed had to earn its place on a phone fast, with as little setup as possible.

Primary research

Secondary data showed the market. To understand the people, I surveyed 103 UK students about how they shop, store, waste and budget for food.

The competitive gap

Plenty of food apps exist. I mapped them by price and by how well they fit student life (tight budgets, shared kitchens, UK shops). One corner was empty.

International meal planners are priced for affluent users and built on US measurements. UK tools like Cherrypick prove grocery integration works, but are aimed at everyone. Banking apps track spend but know nothing about food. None of them handle shared kitchens or ultra-tight budgets.

From insight to product

With the gap defined, I worked feature by feature, and only kept what the research could justify. The empathy map gave me the rule I measured everything against: a maximum of 30 seconds to log an item.

  1. 47% forget what food they already have
    Make the fridge, not a recipe feed, the home screen
    Fridge tracker
  2. 58% want expiry reminders
    Sort by urgency and flag what needs using today
    Freshness status
  3. 46% want recipes from what they have
    Suggest meals built around expiring items first
    AI meal planner
  4. 61% want budget alerts
    Lead with money saved, not just money spent
    Budget dashboard
  5. 71% live in shared houses
    Let flatmates share lists and see who owns what
    Flatmate sharing
  6. 77% of app users gone in three days
    Three tabs only, quick-add logging, celebrate small wins
    30-second logging

Concept: logging in seconds

Logging is the habit everything else depends on. If adding food is a chore, the fridge list goes stale and the meal and budget features stop working. So the add flow is three taps: pick a common item, confirm a suggested use-by date, done.

The tone follows the brand rules: notifications are helpful reminders, not nagging, and the app never makes anyone feel guilty about past habits.

Brand identity

The name joins “fresh” (food, and a new approach) with “track” (staying on top of it). It's short, clear and easy to say, which matters for an audience that picks apps on instinct.

The mark puts a fork and a growing plant inside an open circle: food that stays fresh, and a cycle with nothing wasted.

The personality is friendly, practical and encouraging. Students hear plenty about budgeting and waste already, so the voice is a supportive flatmate rather than a lecturer.

Do Tone of voice
  • Celebrate small wins and progress
  • Show real savings, not features
  • “Food tracking in seconds, not minutes”
Don't Tone of voice
  • Lecture about waste or budgeting
  • Use intimidating financial jargon
  • Make people feel guilty about past habits

Business model

A freemium app fits both the research (76% preferred it) and the market: lifestyle apps convert free users to paid at 23 to 37%, well above the usual 3 to 5%. Retailer commission and university licences add two more revenue streams, so the business doesn't rely on students paying alone.

Reflection

FreshTrack showed me how much stronger design decisions are when they come with evidence attached. Starting from the market and the people, rather than the screens, meant every feature had a reason to exist and a number behind it.

Where it goes next

  • Prototype the add flow and test it with students against the 30-second target, before building anything else.
  • Validate willingness to pay. Preferring freemium isn't the same as upgrading, so the premium tier needs its own test.
  • Design the first three days deliberately, since that's where most apps lose their users.
  • Build privacy in from the start. 67% of people worry about sharing financial data, so trust has to be visible in the product.
